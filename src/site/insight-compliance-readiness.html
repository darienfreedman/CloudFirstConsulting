Make compliance
an operating discipline.

Regulations, contract requirements, security frameworks, certification standards, and architecture models are not interchangeable. The useful starting point is not which product to buy, but which obligations and reference models apply to your organization, information, and services. A dashboard can support that work; it cannot decide applicability or demonstrate that people consistently follow a process.

Establish the obligation before choosing the baseline.

Identify the customers, jurisdictions, contracts, information types, and systems in scope. Confirm the required edition, assessment method, and decision authority with legal, compliance, and procurement stakeholders. Record exclusions and dependencies, including suppliers and administrative access. Different obligations may apply to different parts of the same organization.

Reuse evidence without assuming requirements are equivalent.

Create a control-to-evidence register that links each obligation to an accountable owner, implementation, evidence source, review frequency, and exception process. A joiner-and-leaver procedure or access review may support several requirements, but mappings need review: scope, retention periods, assessment objectives, and approval rules can differ. Record how each requirement is satisfied rather than copying a crosswalk and treating it as proof.

Separate control design from operating effectiveness. A written access policy shows intent; approved access records, completed reviews, and resolved exceptions help show that it operates. Preserve the scope and collection date with evidence, and protect sensitive records used in an assessment.

Use Microsoft capabilities within shared responsibilities.

Purview Compliance Manager can organize assessments, improvement actions, evidence, and Microsoft-managed, customer-managed, and shared controls. Entra can support identity controls; Purview can support information protection and retention; Azure Policy and Defender for Cloud can support configuration and posture review. Coverage depends on the selected services, licenses, configuration, and environment.

A compliance score is a prioritization aid, not a certification or legal conclusion. Review Microsoft's assurance documentation for the exact service, cloud environment, geography, and assessment scope. Your organization still owns relevant policies, personnel processes, access decisions, and operating evidence. Do not assume a commercial-cloud design is automatically suitable for regulated information.

Make readiness sustainable after the assessment.

Start with one bounded information flow and test whether the team can produce the required evidence. Agree a remediation backlog, owners, review dates, and a process for expiring exceptions. Revisit the baseline when contracts, data flows, suppliers, or services change. Useful measures include overdue reviews, evidence freshness, unresolved findings, and time to close exceptions, not just a rising score.

Consulting can help establish scope, map controls, and prepare evidence. It does not replace legal interpretation, an authorized assessment, agency approval, or an independent certification decision, and it does not guarantee compliance.

Security architecture maturity model

CISA Zero Trust Maturity Model

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency Zero Trust Maturity Model 2.0 addresses Identity, Devices, Networks, Applications and Workloads, and Data. Visibility and Analytics, Automation and Orchestration, and Governance span those five pillars.

Use it to sequence improvements across access, device posture, application boundaries, and data protection. It supports federal Zero Trust planning and can inform other sectors, but a maturity assessment is not a certification or an automatic mandate for every organization.

Industry examples

  • Accounting and Advisory: Govern access across client teams
  • Architecture and Engineering: Manage external design-team access
  • Construction and real estate: Secure project-office and site-administration devices

Criminal-justice information security policy

CJIS Security Policy v6.0 reference

The FBI Criminal Justice Information Services Security Policy addresses protection of criminal justice information. Version 6.0 is a reference baseline, not a claim that it remains the latest governing revision. Confirm the applicable version, agency requirements, agreements, and personnel and access conditions with the responsible authority.

Apply it where criminal justice information is in scope, not automatically to every public-sector workload. Cloud suitability depends on covered services, agreements, architecture, and customer-operated controls. No generic government-cloud label or universal Microsoft compliance score replaces agency approval and operating evidence.

Contract-driven cybersecurity assessment program

CMMC 2.0 and defense-supply-chain readiness

Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification addresses protection of federal contract information and controlled unclassified information in the defense supply chain. Required levels, assessment routes, and timing depend on current program rules and contract clauses, not simply membership in an industry.

Identify in-scope information, systems, suppliers, and evidence before planning an assessment. The published Level 2 baseline references NIST SP 800-171 Revision 2; publication of Revision 3 does not automatically change a contract's baseline. Verify current program guidance and assessment requirements.

European Union AI regulation

EU AI Act and risk-based AI Governance

The EU AI Act sets requirements according to intended use and the responsibilities of providers, deployers, and other actors. Assess prohibited uses, high-risk requirements, transparency duties, and applicable general-purpose AI model obligations rather than treating every assistant as the same risk category.

Determine territorial scope, organizational role, use classification, and applicable effective dates before deployment. Record oversight, evaluation, documentation, and incident responsibilities where required. NIST AI RMF can inform risk work but does not itself establish compliance with the Act.

European Union data-protection regulation

General Data Protection Regulation

The General Data Protection Regulation establishes obligations for personal-data processing within its territorial scope. Controller and processor roles, lawful basis, purpose limitation, data minimization, rights, security, and accountability must be considered together.

Confirm applicability with privacy counsel, including relevant processing by organizations outside the European Union. Map data flows, retention, requests, transfers, and incident procedures. A privacy tool or consent checkbox does not establish every lawful basis or satisfy every obligation.

GDPR: official regulation text

Industry examples

  • Accounting and Advisory: Protect client financial records
  • Automotive and mobility: Govern mobility and commercial analytics
  • Education: Protect student records in collaboration

Certifiable management-system standard

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 information security management

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 defines requirements for an information security management system, including leadership, risk treatment, performance evaluation, and continual improvement. Certification concerns a defined organizational scope, not an isolated product or a list of enabled settings.

Connect information risks, control ownership, documented operating evidence, internal audits, and corrective actions. A cloud provider's certification does not certify your organization or every workload you deploy; confirm the applicable edition and assessment scope.

Voluntary AI risk framework

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 uses Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage to address risks to people, organizations, and society throughout the AI lifecycle. It supports context, accountable ownership, evaluation, and ongoing risk decisions rather than prescribing one product stack.

Use for assistants, agents, and custom AI applications, with evaluation tied to intended use and affected people. It is voluntary guidance, not proof of compliance with an AI law or a substitute for professional judgment.

Cybersecurity risk framework

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

The National Institute of Standards and Technology Cybersecurity Framework organizes outcomes across Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. Use current and target profiles to connect business priorities to security improvements; its implementation tiers describe risk-governance and management practices, not a certification score.

Useful across industries for risk discussions, response readiness, recovery planning, and supplier oversight. Select supporting controls separately: NIST SP 800-53 and SP 800-171 have different purposes and are not interchangeable with the CSF.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework

Payment-card security standard

PCI DSS 4.0 family and payment-data protection

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard applies to entities that store, process, or transmit cardholder data or sensitive authentication data, and systems that can affect the cardholder data environment. The 4.0 family includes the 4.0.1 update; confirm the currently applicable version and validation requirements.

Map payment flows, service-provider responsibilities, segmentation, access, logging, and evidence with payment stakeholders. Outsourcing payments may reduce scope but does not automatically eliminate obligations. A generic retail or finance workload is not necessarily a payment-card environment.

PCI Security Standards Council: PCI DSS

Industry examples

  • Nonprofits: Plan continuity for donation and program systems
  • Retail and consumer goods: Standardize store identity and device controls
  • Travel and hospitality: Modernize customer-facing business platforms

Industrial reference architecture

Purdue Model for OT/ICS architecture

The Purdue Model helps describe operational technology (OT) and industrial control systems (ICS), from physical processes and control through operations management and enterprise business systems, commonly using levels 0 through 5. It is a reference for discussing system roles and boundaries, not a regulation or a complete modern security architecture.

Use it to map approved data flows, remote-access paths, and separation between business and operational technology. Apply risk-based zones and conduits, including ISA/IEC 62443 concepts where appropriate, with industrial specialists. Cloud connectivity does not remove safety, availability, or separately authorized assessment requirements.

NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3: operational technology security
